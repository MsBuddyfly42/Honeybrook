COURSE 101 · LESSON ONE

How a shot tells a story

A shot is a continuous piece of film between cuts. The filmmaker chooses what appears in the frame, where the camera sits, and how long we see it. Those choices guide our attention and influence how we feel.

Start with distance

An extreme long shot can show a person in a wide landscape. A long shot shows the whole person and their surroundings. A medium shot often shows a person from around the waist up. A close-up brings a face or object near, making a reaction or detail easier to notice.

Then look at the angle

A camera at eye level looks approximately straight at its subject. A high angle looks down; a low angle looks up. These can suggest vulnerability or power, but their effect depends on the scene. Avoid assuming one angle always means the same thing.

Look at lighting and sound

Bright, even lighting can make a space feel open; strong shadows can hide information or create uncertainty. Diegetic sound belongs to the story world, such as a character playing a piano. A musical score heard by the audience but not the characters is generally nondiegetic.

Put the choices together

Imagine a bear waiting alone at a theater door. A wide shot shows the empty street; a close-up shows a trembling paw; a quiet footstep behind the bear changes what we expect. Describe what you observe first, then explain how the combination affects you. Another viewer may interpret it differently.

HANDS-ON PRACTICE · CAMERA FRAMING

Work at the camera table

Try framing the same Honeybrook classroom in two ways. This exercise simulates a crop of one photograph; it does not move a real camera or change the angle.

  1. Look at the scene in the viewfinder.
  2. Select Wide view to see the room, then select Closer view to emphasize a smaller area.
  3. Move the horizontal position slider and notice how the frame changes.
  4. Write what you notice in your assignment below. Your choices can be changed whenever you like.

Wide view: the room and its surroundings are visible.

Your assignment: Read a scene

Choose any scene you can access, or use the imaginary theater-door scene above. This exercise is a written analysis; you do not need to upload a film.

  1. Pick a scene. Choose a short scene from a film you already have access to, or use the bear at the theater door described above. You do not need to upload a film.
  2. Watch or read once. Ask yourself: Who is present, where are they, and what happens? Click the box below and write one or two sentences answering those questions.
  3. Look at the camera. Watch again or reread the example. Is it a wide view, a medium shot, or a close-up? Does the camera look up, down, or straight ahead? Write the choice you noticed and describe what you can see.
  4. Listen and look for light. Write down a sound or a lighting detail, such as footsteps or deep shadows. Say when you noticed it.
  5. Explain the effect. Describe the mood you felt and explain how the camera and the sound or lighting helped create it. Use your own interpretation; different answers can be reasonable.
  6. Review your work. Use the checklist below. Your writing saves automatically in this browser as you type; look for “Saved on this browser.”
Example and self-review guide

Example: “A bear waits outside an empty theater. A wide shot shows the quiet street, and a close-up shows the bear’s trembling paw. Footsteps behind the bear make me expect someone to appear. These choices make the scene feel suspenseful.”

Check your own work for four things: a description of the scene; a named camera choice and visible detail; a sound or lighting detail; and an explanation connecting those choices to the mood. The written assignment is for self-review and does not receive an automatic score.

Your analysis stays here as a draft. You can return and revise it; there is no submit button or automatic essay grade in this first lesson.

HANDS-ON PRACTICE · EDITING ROOM

Build a three-shot sequence

Arrange three still photographs as a simple storyboard. You can change the order and play a timed sequence to see how the order affects the story. These are still images, not recorded video clips.

  1. Use Move earlier or Move later to change the order of the shots.
  2. Select how long each shot stays on screen.
  3. Choose Play sequence to watch your edit.
  4. Try a different order and compare how the scene feels. Your arrangement saves on this browser.
